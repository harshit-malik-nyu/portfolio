Project one · financial crime compliance

Where should a bank set its sanctions threshold?

$15.6m

the annual swing a single threshold decides

there isn't one — and that is the finding. No threshold makes name-only screening both staffable and adequately sensitive, because 15% of published aliases share no characters with any other name for the same party. The investment case is secondary identifiers, not threshold tuning or better matching software.

Situation. Every customer and payment at a large bank is screened against sanctions lists. Published benchmarks put 90–95% of the resulting alerts in the false-positive column, each costing roughly twenty minutes of analyst time.

Complication. The threshold controlling that volume is set by judgement. Lower it and the queue becomes unstaffable; raise it and designated parties pass through. No public measurement exists of the trade-off, because the ground truth is confidential.

Project two · AI automation economics

What does an AI agent actually cost to run?

0.38%

of an AI automation budget is the model

inference is 0.38% of the bill. Human review is the rest. And the review rate is set by whether the agent can tell when it is wrong — not by how often it is right. A 95%-accurate agent that cannot rank its own errors costs six times more to run than an 85%-accurate agent that can.

Situation. Every large organisation is now costing AI automation of back-office document work — contract review, claims triage, invoice matching. The comparison is almost always framed as model capability against price per token.

Complication. Nobody publishes the unit economics. The vendor pitch optimises the one term that turns out not to matter, and the term that does matter is not accuracy.

Project three · professional services risk

Do the citations in this report exist?

13%

of citations in honest professional work do not resolve

13% of author-written citations do not resolve, and the rate does not depend on who wrote them. Experts and non-experts fail identically; machine-deposited references fail at 0.0%. The control that works is requiring an identifier at the point of writing, not reviewing harder afterwards.

Situation. Deloitte, EY and KPMG have each had work retracted or refunded after reports were found to cite papers that were never written. The reputational exposure runs well past the fee.

Complication. Nobody knew how often citations fail to resolve in ordinary, honest work — so a firm finding three bad references had no way to know whether that was alarming or normal.

Question. What is the base rate, and what does it take to catch a fabrication against it?

The impact, and for whom

To a firm's risk and quality partner

To an engagement manager before delivery

A 13% base rate means three unresolvable references in a report is normal, not alarming — which is the number that tells you when to worry and when not to. Without it, every finding looks like a scandal or gets waved through.

Project four · AI evaluation

Can anyone tell when a model gets worse?

6 of 9

benchmarks cannot detect the gains announced on them

most cannot. Six of nine widely quoted benchmarks cannot separate a two-point change from noise. Correct for the fact that questions cluster by subject, and none of them can. The implication is not better tuning — it is that a large share of reported model progress is unmeasurable at the sample sizes in use.

Situation. Billions in capital allocation, procurement and safety certification now rest on benchmark scores. Model announcements quote improvements of one to three points as evidence of progress.

Complication. A benchmark is a measuring instrument with a resolution, set almost entirely by how many questions it asks. Below that resolution it returns noise. Nobody publishes the resolution.

The impact, and for whom

To a lab choosing what to ship

A regression detector fires on noise or misses real degradation depending on a threshold nobody has calibrated. A missed two-point regression costs roughly $8,400 in degraded output before anything else surfaces it; investigating a false alarm costs $4,500 in engineering time. The ratio is about two to one, not the orders of magnitude the field assumes.

To anyone buying on benchmark scores

Procurement and capability claims rest on differences the instrument cannot resolve. The check runs in a build pipeline with no model calls and exits non-zero when a reported improvement exceeds what the benchmark can measure.

Immediate action: run every benchmark in your eval suite through the size check. Any that cannot resolve the differences you report on them should carry an interval, or be replaced.

Project five · organisational design and AI risk

How you evaluate reviewers decides what your AI gets wrong

832,000

manual reviews per million decisions, caused by an accountability rule rather than by the model

Outcome-based accountability does not merely break the safety guarantee — it destroys the automation case. When reviewers are judged on whether anything went wrong, they check the cases the model is confident about, and the statistical correction restores the error rate while leaving throughput gone.

Situation. Organisations deploying AI with human oversight calibrate a confidence threshold: above it, decisions pass automatically; below it, a person reviews.

Complication. The reviewer sees that same confidence score, and what they choose to check changes which cases reach production. The guarantee is calibrated on a population the deployment then alters.

Question. Does the guarantee survive contact with the people it was built for?

The impact, and for whom

To an operations leader deploying AI oversight

The threshold is not the lever. How reviewers are evaluated changes deployed error rate and throughput by more than any model choice — 89% of volume automated under one accountability rule against 17% under another, same model, same cases, same people.

To whoever signs off on the risk

A guarantee measured in the lab fails in 90% of deployments once humans are in the loop. The correction works and has a boundary: past a point it stops failing visibly and starts quietly refusing to automate anything, so monitoring belongs on throughput, not on error rate.

Immediate action: before tuning a confidence threshold, find out what your reviewers actually check and how they are appraised. The second question changes the answer more than the first.

The detectable cases are the ones that did not need detecting. A flood that swamps a docket announces itself. A campaign sized to be the largest bloc without being conspicuous costs 36 cents to produce and roughly $1,800 of audit time to find — and that gap widens as it gets smaller.

Situation. A federal agency proposing a rule must consider the public comments it receives. In 2017 the FCC's net neutrality docket drew 22.1 million; the New York Attorney General later found roughly 18 million were fabricated, filed under names of people who had not submitted them.

Complication. Regulations.gov allows 1,000 requests an hour and comment text costs one request each. Reading a million-comment docket in full is forty days of continuous polling. Nobody reads these dockets — not journalists, not researchers, not the agency.

Project nine · AI benchmark validity

The leaderboard holds. Except where it doesn't.

1 in 1,723

model rankings that reverse when the benchmark is swapped for a held-out replica

Leaderboards are more robust than the contamination debate suggests — and the exceptions are not random. Across 63 models from twenty labs, only one ordering separated by five points or more flips. The largest is a model losing a 7.2-point lead and finishing behind, an 8.2-point swing that holds at p = 0.0001.

Situation. Companies choose models from leaderboards. Procurement, architecture, and standardisation decisions all trace back to a benchmark table.

Complication. The research literature asks whether a model is contaminated — unanswerable from outside, and a 2026 result found ten detection methods scoring near chance on reasoning models.

Question. Ask the decision-grade version instead: not whether a score is inflated, but whether the ranking survives. If the table reorders, whose training data caused it stops mattering.

The impact, and for whom

To anyone choosing a model from a benchmark table

The ordering is largely safe. Where it is not, it fails between a model suspected of memorising the benchmark and one that was not — so the pairs worth re-checking are identifiable in advance, rather than requiring every score to be distrusted.

To anyone running an evaluation

A rank change is an interaction and needs roughly four times the sample of a score change the same size. Most published comparisons are powered for the second and quoted for the first.

Immediate action: before accepting a leaderboard gap, check whether the pair you care about is separated by more than twice the standard error. Below that they were never ranked, and the ordering is a coin landing.

The benchmark changes the numbers in each question and recomputes the answer through the dataset's own arithmetic, so every variant is correct by construction rather than by argument.
