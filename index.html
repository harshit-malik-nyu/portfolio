Project one · AI evaluation

Can anyone tell when a model gets worse?

Situation. Billions in capital allocation, procurement and safety certification now rest on benchmark scores. Model announcements quote improvements of one to three points as evidence of progress.

Complication. A benchmark is a measuring instrument with a resolution, set almost entirely by how many questions it asks. Below that resolution it returns noise. Nobody publishes the resolution.

Question. Which benchmarks can actually support the claims being made on them?

Answer: most cannot. Six of nine widely quoted benchmarks cannot separate a two-point change from noise. Correct for the fact that questions cluster by subject, and none of them can. The implication is not better tuning — it is that a large share of reported model progress is unmeasurable at the sample sizes in use.

What it's worth, and to whom

To a lab choosing what to ship

A regression detector fires on noise or misses real degradation depending on a threshold nobody has calibrated. A missed two-point regression costs roughly $8,400 in degraded output before anything else surfaces it; investigating a false alarm costs $4,500 in engineering time. The ratio is about two to one, not the orders of magnitude the field assumes.

To anyone buying on benchmark scores

Procurement and capability claims rest on differences the instrument cannot resolve. The check runs in a build pipeline with no model calls and exits non-zero when a reported improvement exceeds what the benchmark can measure.

Do this Monday: run every benchmark in your eval suite through the size check. Any that cannot resolve the differences you report on them should carry an interval, or be replaced.

Partway through, I found concurrent work that does this more rigorously and says so on the front page. It also showed my own measurement instrument was aimed at the least consequential source of error. Both are written up in the repository.

Project two · financial crime compliance

Where should a bank set its sanctions threshold?

Situation. Every customer and payment at a large bank is screened against sanctions lists. Published benchmarks put 90–95% of the resulting alerts in the false-positive column, each costing roughly twenty minutes of analyst time.

Complication. The threshold controlling that volume is set by judgement. Lower it and the queue becomes unstaffable; raise it and designated parties pass through. No public measurement exists of the trade-off, because the ground truth is confidential.

Question. What is the defensible operating point, and what does being wrong cost in each direction?

Answer: there isn't one — and that is the finding. No threshold makes name-only screening both staffable and adequately sensitive, because 15% of published aliases share no characters with any other name for the same party. The investment case is secondary identifiers, not threshold tuning or better matching software.

What it's worth, and to whom

To the head of financial crime compliance

At a mid-tier bank's volume, the threshold decides a swing of $15.6m to $90k in annual review cost, and 159 analysts to one. That range is the real decision, and it is currently made by judgement with no measurement behind it.

To whoever signs the technology budget

Vendor spend on better fuzzy matching buys movement along a frontier that stops at 75.9% recall. 15.1% of published aliases are unreachable by any name-matching method, so that money is capped by construction. Identifier capture moves the frontier; matching software does not.

Do this Monday: stop the threshold review. Price mandatory date-of-birth and registration-number capture at onboarding instead, and measure the alert reduction before renewing the screening vendor.

Project three · professional services risk

Do the citations in this report exist?

Situation. Deloitte, EY and KPMG have each had work retracted or refunded after reports were found to cite papers that were never written. The reputational exposure runs well past the fee.

Complication. Nobody knew how often citations fail to resolve in ordinary, honest work — so a firm finding three bad references had no way to know whether that was alarming or normal.

Question. What is the base rate, and what does it take to catch a fabrication against it?

Answer: 13% of author-written citations do not resolve, and the rate does not depend on who wrote them. Experts and non-experts fail identically; machine-deposited references fail at 0.0%. The control that works is requiring an identifier at the point of writing, not reviewing harder afterwards.

What it's worth, and to whom

To a firm's risk and quality partner

One retracted report costs roughly A$732,000 — fee refund, remediation, and the commercial drag of a public correction. Running this check across every deliverable costs about A$35,000 a year. The case does not need the incident rate to be high; it needs it to be non-zero.

To an engagement manager before delivery

A 13% base rate means three unresolvable references in a report is normal, not alarming — which is the number that tells you when to worry and when not to. Without it, every finding looks like a scandal or gets waved through.

Do this Monday: require a DOI or stable identifier at the point of writing. Deposited references fail at 0.0% against 13% for described ones — the control belongs upstream, not in review.

What the three have in common

Different industries, the same failure. In each case a number was being used to make decisions, it looked reasonable, and it was wrong in a way the summary statistics could not show.

The aggregate never revealed it

Every error was found by reading individual rows. Four corrections to one headline — 37.9%, 52.3%, 57.3%, 75.9% — and not one of the intermediate figures looked suspicious.

Ground truth came from construction

None of the three relies on labelled data or on my judgement. The labels follow from how the data was assembled, so a reader can check them without trusting me.

Each argues against itself

Every repository contains a document making the strongest case against its own conclusion, including the objections I could not answer.
